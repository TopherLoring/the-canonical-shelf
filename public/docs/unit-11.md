## Speak carefully about God

**Objective:** Distinguish Trinity, incarnation, and providence while recognizing the limits of explanatory models.

### Matthew 28:16–20 · Berean Standard Bible

**16** Meanwhile, the eleven disciples went to Galilee, to the mountain Jesus had designated.

**17** When they saw Him, they worshiped Him, but some doubted.

**18** Then Jesus came to them and said, “All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Me.

**19** Therefore go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit,

**20** and teaching them to obey all that I have commanded you. And surely I am with you always, even to the end of the age.”

Christian doctrine gathers and interprets biblical testimony in a disciplined way. The Trinity names the historic confession of one God in three persons: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. It does not mean three separate gods or one person merely switching roles. The language aims to preserve unity and real distinction together.

Incarnation names the Son’s becoming human in Jesus Christ. Historic Christianity confesses Jesus as fully divine and fully human. These claims should not be explained as a half-divine, half-human mixture. John’s language about the Word and Philippians’ account of self-giving are important primary readings for examining the confession.

The Spirit is described in relation to life, prayer, community, and God’s work among people. Romans 8 places divine help alongside human weakness and hope. Understanding the Spirit does not require learners to treat every strong feeling as a direct command from God. Discernment and responsibility remain important.

Providence concerns God’s purposes and care within history. Our guide teaches meaningful human response: people can accept or resist grace, while their choices cannot ultimately thwart God’s purpose. Foreknowledge means knowing beforehand; predestination concerns an appointed outcome; determinism concerns how events are determined. They are not interchangeable terms.

Analogies can open understanding but also mislead. Water changing states can obscure the distinction of persons; dividing an object into thirds can suggest that each person is only part of God. State the doctrine first, identify an analogy’s limits, and leave room for mystery without using mystery as an excuse for careless reasoning. Understanding is assessed; assent is not required for participation.

**Simply:** Trinity concerns who God is; incarnation concerns the Son becoming human; providence concerns God’s purposes in history. Keep the questions distinct.

### Vocabulary

- **Trinity:** The historic confession of one God in three persons.
- **Incarnation:** The Son’s becoming human in Jesus Christ.
- **Providence:** God’s care and purposes within the world’s history.

**Deeper:** Compare what a biblical passage directly says with how a later doctrinal formulation draws several passages together. Both deserve careful study, but they are not identical literary acts.

**Optional reflection:** What makes an analogy helpful, and when should it be set aside?

**Model reflection:** It can clarify one feature, but it should be set aside when its other features contradict the claim it was meant to explain.

## Trinity: one God, real distinction

**Objective:** Distinguish the historic Trinity from three gods, three parts, and one person changing roles.

### Matthew 28:16–20 · Berean Standard Bible

**16** Meanwhile, the eleven disciples went to Galilee, to the mountain Jesus had designated.

**17** When they saw Him, they worshiped Him, but some doubted.

**18** Then Jesus came to them and said, “All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Me.

**19** Therefore go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit,

**20** and teaching them to obey all that I have commanded you. And surely I am with you always, even to the end of the age.”

The closing commission names Father, Son, and Holy Spirit together in baptism. Read what the passage says before moving to later terminology. Historic Trinitarian teaching draws this testimony together with other biblical passages rather than pretending a complete later definition appears in one sentence.

The historic confession holds that God is one and that Father, Son, and Spirit are distinct persons. Person here does not simply mean three separate human individuals enlarged to cosmic size. The language protects distinction without dividing God into three competing beings.

Calling each person one third of God fails to express the confession. Describing one person changing costumes also fails, because it removes real distinction. These are useful near-miss comparisons: identifying why an explanation fails can be clearer than adding an elaborate analogy.

A beginner can learn the boundaries of the teaching without claiming to make God fully comprehensible. Mystery names the limits of understanding; it should not be used to avoid distinguishing a coherent formulation from its alternatives. The mission checks that distinction, not personal assent.

**Simply:** Historic Christianity confesses one God and three distinct persons. It does not mean three gods, three pieces, or one person in three costumes.

### Vocabulary

- **Trinity:** The historic confession of one God in three persons.
- **Distinction:** A real difference that need not imply separation into three gods.
- **Doctrinal synthesis:** A formulation drawing several biblical testimonies together.

**Deeper:** An analogy can illustrate one feature while distorting another. State which feature it helps explain and exactly where it fails.

**Optional reflection:** How would you correct a misleading analogy without ridiculing the learner?

**Model reflection:** I could say that the costume image conveys unity but loses the distinction of persons. I would return to the definition rather than keep adding costumes.

## Incarnation: fully divine and fully human

**Objective:** Explain incarnation without reducing Jesus to a divine disguise or a half-human mixture.

John begins with the Word in relation to God and then speaks of the Word becoming flesh. The movement holds divine identity and embodied life together. Flesh should not be treated as an unimportant outer costume in this account.

Historic Christianity confesses Jesus as fully divine and fully human. The claim is not that two incomplete halves combine to form an unusual third kind of being. Nor does it mean the human life is only a visual performance without genuine human experience.

The passage links Jesus with grace, truth, and making God known. Incarnation concerns revelation as well as presence: Christians look to Jesus to understand God’s character. The course reads human life, suffering, and self-giving in light of this confession.

Compare John’s language with Philippians 2:5–11 in the unit overview. Ask what each presentation emphasizes before constructing a summary. A disciplined explanation preserves both humanity and divinity instead of using one to cancel the other whenever a question becomes difficult.

**Simply:** Incarnation means the Son truly becomes human. Historic teaching keeps both full humanity and full divinity in view.

## The Spirit: help, character, and discernment

**Objective:** Connect teaching about the Spirit with observable character while avoiding certainty based only on intensity of feeling.

### Galatians 5:16–26 · Berean Standard Bible

**16** So I say, walk by the Spirit, and you will not gratify the desires of the flesh.

**17** For the flesh craves what is contrary to the Spirit, and the Spirit what is contrary to the flesh. They are opposed to each other, so that you do not do what you want.

**18** But if you are led by the Spirit, you are not under the law.

**19** The acts of the flesh are obvious: sexual immorality, impurity, and debauchery;

**20** idolatry and sorcery; hatred, discord, jealousy, and rage; rivalries, divisions, factions,

**21** and envy; drunkenness, orgies, and the like. I warn you, as I did before, that those who practice such things will not inherit the kingdom of God.

**22** But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness,

**23** gentleness, and self-control. Against such things there is no law.

**24** Those who belong to Christ Jesus have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires.

**25** Since we live by the Spirit, let us walk in step with the Spirit.

**26** Let us not become conceited, provoking and envying one another.

Galatians describes conflict in human conduct and contrasts destructive works with the fruit of the Spirit. The metaphor of fruit directs attention to what a life produces. It is not merely an inventory of dramatic spiritual experiences.

The listed qualities include love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. Different translations use different English terms, so compare wording without assuming the moral concern changes whenever a word changes.

The warning against conceit, provocation, and envy keeps the passage communal. Spiritual language does not excuse contempt for neighbors. A claim to guidance should be examined alongside conduct, responsibility, and the wider scriptural conversation.

This does not turn a list of virtues into a salvation score or make every difficult emotion a spiritual failure. Growth can include admitting harm, seeking help, and practicing a different response. The Spirit’s work is taught alongside human weakness and dependence, including Romans 8 in this unit.

**Simply:** Look at what a claimed spiritual influence produces in conduct. Intensity of feeling alone does not establish its source.

- **Fruit of the Spirit:** The qualities of life associated with the Spirit in Galatians 5.
- **Self-control:** Responsible regulation of one’s actions rather than domination of others.

**Deeper:** Compare the fruit list with the final warning about conceit and envy. The list should not be used as a reason to boast about spiritual superiority.

## Providence: purpose without erasing response

**Objective:** Distinguish providence, foreknowledge, and determinism within the guide’s account of meaningful response.

### Romans 8:26–30 · Berean Standard Bible

**26** In the same way, the Spirit helps us in our weakness. For we do not know how we ought to pray, but the Spirit Himself intercedes for us with groans too deep for words.

**27** And He who searches our hearts knows the mind of the Spirit, because the Spirit intercedes for the saints according to the will of God.

**28** And we know that God works all things together for the good of those who love Him, who are called according to His purpose.

**29** For those God foreknew, He also predestined to be conformed to the image of His Son, so that He would be the firstborn among many brothers.

**30** And those He predestined, He also called; those He called, He also justified; those He justified, He also glorified.

Romans places God’s help within weakness and hope. The passage does not begin with humans understanding every event. The Spirit’s intercession is part of its account of divine involvement in lives that remain limited and vulnerable.

The claim about God working for good must not be turned into the claim that every harmful act is itself good. The following language relates God’s purpose to conformity to the Son. A promise about divine purpose is not permission to call cruelty necessary or desirable.

Foreknowledge concerns knowing beforehand; predestination concerns an appointed end; determinism concerns what determines events. Christians relate these differently. This guide teaches meaningful acceptance or resistance to grace while affirming that human choices cannot ultimately thwart God’s purpose.

Knowing an outcome and causing every choice are different claims. Likewise, God bringing good from an event does not remove an actor’s responsibility for harm. Explain these distinctions without claiming the passage gives a complete philosophical model or a specific diagnosis of every tragedy.

**Simply:** God’s purpose and human responsibility are both taken seriously. Knowing, causing, and bringing good from an event are different claims.

### Vocabulary

- **Providence:** God’s care and purposes within the world and history.
- **Foreknowledge:** Knowing beforehand, a concept distinct from causing.
- **Determinism:** An account in which events are determined by prior causes or conditions.

**Deeper:** Compare the passage’s stated purpose with the outcomes readers sometimes promise on its behalf. It does not guarantee that every personal preference will be fulfilled.

**Optional reflection:** How would you preserve hope without calling someone’s harm good?

**Model reflection:** I could affirm the possibility of healing and new good while still naming the original action as wrong and supporting accountability.
