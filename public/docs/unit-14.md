## Stay honest with difficult questions

**Objective:** Address suffering, difficult ethical texts, and religious difference with evidence and care.

### Luke 13:1–5 · Berean Standard Bible

**1** At that time some of those present told Jesus about the Galileans whose blood Pilate had mixed with their sacrifices.

**2** To this He replied, “Do you think that these Galileans were worse sinners than all the other Galileans, because they suffered this way?

**3** No, I tell you. But unless you repent, you too will all perish.

**4** Or those eighteen who were killed when the tower of Siloam collapsed on them: Do you think that they were more sinful than all the others living in Jerusalem?

**5** No, I tell you. But unless you repent, you too will all perish.”

Questions about suffering and evil are both intellectual and personal. Luke 13 challenges the inference that victims suffered because they were worse sinners than others. Our guide distinguishes wrongdoing enabled by human freedom from disease and disasters within a dynamic natural world. It does not claim a complete answer to why God permits every loss.

God can bring healing and good from suffering without suffering becoming good in itself or every tragedy being a deliberate lesson. Lament, uncertainty, practical help, and hope belong together. Biblical miracles can be studied seriously alongside differing interpretations. Neither a diagnosis nor an unhealed condition establishes possession or inadequate faith.

Conquest, slavery, and restrictions on women require direct examination rather than avoidance. Philemon can be studied for Paul’s appeal to receive Onesimus as a brother, while also asking what the letter explicitly says and what it leaves unresolved about slavery. Historical context does not by itself excuse harm or settle every moral question.

The guide explicitly affirms LGBTQ dignity, relationships, marriage, and equal participation in ministry. Opposing interpretations and affirming responses should be explained with attention to context; people’s dignity is not treated as an open contest. Present-day application is examined through Jesus’ teaching, love of neighbor, and human dignity.

Respectful treatment of other religions includes Christianity’s Jewish roots and the rejection of collective Jewish blame for Jesus’ death. Romans 11 challenges a simple claim that God abandoned Israel. Teach salvation through Christ while explaining disagreements about the unevangelized and covenant without declaring particular people’s final destiny. Acknowledging uncertainty is more responsible than inventing certainty.

**Simply:** Read difficult texts directly, acknowledge what remains unresolved, and keep people’s dignity and actual needs in view.

### Vocabulary

- **Theodicy:** An attempt to account for evil and suffering in relation to God.
- **Interpretive boundary:** A limit on what the available evidence establishes.
- **Religious literacy:** Accurate understanding of beliefs and practices, including their diversity.

**Deeper:** For a difficult passage, list its explicit statements, your interpretation, the moral question, and the proposed application separately. This makes disagreement easier to examine without disguising it.

**Optional reflection:** How would you respond to someone who has been harmed by a religious explanation of their suffering?

**Model reflection:** I would listen without diagnosing their faith, acknowledge the harm, and offer practical support rather than impose another unearned certainty.

## Difficult ethical texts: neither avoidance nor easy excuses

**Objective:** Distinguish a text’s explicit appeal, historical setting, and unresolved ethical questions.

### Philemon 1:8–21 · Berean Standard Bible

**8** So although in Christ I am bold enough to order you to do what is proper,

**9** I prefer to appeal on the basis of love. For I, Paul, am now aged, and a prisoner of Christ Jesus as well.

**10** I appeal to you for my child Onesimus, whose father I became while I was in chains.

**11** Formerly he was useless to you, but now he has become useful both to you and to me.

**12** I am sending back to you him who is my very heart.

**13** I would have liked to keep him with me, so that on your behalf he could minister to me in my chains for the gospel.

**14** But I did not want to do anything without your consent, so that your goodness will not be out of compulsion, but by your own free will.

**15** For perhaps this is why he was separated from you for a while, so that you might have him back for good—

**16** no longer as a slave, but better than a slave, as a beloved brother. He is especially beloved to me, but even more so to you, both in person and in the Lord.

**17** So if you consider me a partner, receive him as you would receive me.

**18** But if he has wronged you in any way or owes you anything, charge it to my account.

**19** I, Paul, write this with my own hand. I will repay it—not to mention that you owe me your very self.

**20** Yes, brother, let me have some benefit from you in the Lord. Refresh my heart in Christ.

**21** Confident of your obedience, I write to you, knowing that you will do even more than I ask.

Paul appeals to Philemon concerning Onesimus through the language of love, relationship, and reception as a brother. Read the appeal’s actual wording rather than immediately replacing it with either a modern abolition statement or a defense of slavery.

The letter operates within a world in which slavery existed. That historical fact helps explain the situation but does not make slavery morally acceptable. Context explains a setting; it is not a machine for turning harm into goodness.

The appeal can be examined for how it changes the relationship it addresses and for what it leaves unstated. Christians have used biblical texts in opposing ways concerning slavery. A responsible reading must confront the harm of oppressive uses rather than claiming the interpretive history is uncomplicated.

Use the same method with conquest and restrictions on women: record explicit claims, examine context and competing readings, name the moral problem, and evaluate an application through Jesus’ teaching and human dignity. Some questions remain difficult after careful study. Honesty about those limits is part of the work.

**Simply:** Read what the text says and what it leaves unresolved. Understanding an ancient setting does not require excusing its harms.

### Vocabulary

- **Historical context:** The circumstances in which a text and its audience are situated.
- **Ethical evaluation:** Judgment about the rightness and effects of an action.
- **Interpretive limit:** A boundary beyond which the available evidence does not settle a claim.

**Deeper:** Philemon’s appeal and modern abolition are not identical statements. Examine the relationship between them without claiming the letter explicitly answers every later question.

**Optional reflection:** What would an honest explanation of a difficult text need to acknowledge?

**Model reflection:** It would name the troubling issue, summarize the actual appeal, and distinguish a defensible implication from an explicit statement.

## Jewish roots and respectful religious difference

**Objective:** Reject a simple abandonment account of Israel while distinguishing Christian witness from certainty about individuals.

### Romans 11:1–6 · Berean Standard Bible

**1** I ask then, did God reject His people? Certainly not! I am an Israelite myself, a descendant of Abraham, from the tribe of Benjamin.

**2** God did not reject His people, whom He foreknew. Do you not know what the Scripture says about Elijah, how he appealed to God against Israel:

**3** “Lord, they have killed Your prophets and torn down Your altars. I am the only one left, and they are seeking my life as well” ?

**4** And what was the divine reply to him? “I have reserved for Myself seven thousand men who have not bowed the knee to Baal.”

**5** In the same way, at the present time there is a remnant chosen by grace.

**6** And if it is by grace, then it is no longer by works. Otherwise, grace would no longer be grace.

Paul asks whether God has rejected his people and answers negatively. His own identity is part of the response. This is an important obstacle to a simplistic story in which Christianity appears only after God discards Israel.

Jesus, Paul, and the earliest Christian movement belong within Jewish history. Judaism also continues as a living tradition with its own interpretation of Scripture. It should not be presented merely as a failed precursor whose only purpose was to prepare for someone else’s religion.

Christian teaching about salvation through Christ raises real questions about covenant and those of other faiths. Christians answer those questions differently. This guide teaches Christ’s saving significance without assigning a known final destiny to particular people or presenting personal speculation about repeated lives as doctrine.

Respectful witness includes accurate description, listening, and rejection of collective Jewish blame for Jesus’ death. A disagreement about truth does not authorize contempt or the erasure of a community’s own account of itself. Begin by learning what another person actually believes.

**Simply:** Christianity has Jewish roots, and Paul rejects a simple claim that God discarded Israel. Religious difference calls for accuracy and respect.

### Vocabulary

- **Covenant:** A committed relationship involving divine promise and communal responsibility.
- **Remnant:** A remaining group within a wider people, a theme used in several biblical settings.
- **Witness:** Communicating one’s faith through words and conduct without claiming control of another’s response.

**Deeper:** Read Romans 9–11 as a connected argument before using one sentence to settle every question about Israel. This lesson establishes a boundary against simplistic abandonment, not a complete covenant theory.

## Suffering, miracles, and spiritual discernment

**Objective:** Distinguish theological questions from unsupported judgments about victims or illness.

### Luke 13:1–5 · Berean Standard Bible

**1** At that time some of those present told Jesus about the Galileans whose blood Pilate had mixed with their sacrifices.

**2** To this He replied, “Do you think that these Galileans were worse sinners than all the other Galileans, because they suffered this way?

**3** No, I tell you. But unless you repent, you too will all perish.

**4** Or those eighteen who were killed when the tower of Siloam collapsed on them: Do you think that they were more sinful than all the others living in Jerusalem?

**5** No, I tell you. But unless you repent, you too will all perish.”

Jesus challenges the inference that people who suffered particular disasters were worse sinners than others. The examples include human violence and a falling tower. The passage does not give a complete theory of suffering, but it directly resists a particular way of blaming victims.

This guide distinguishes moral harm arising through human choices from disease and disasters within a dynamic natural world. God’s ability to bring good from suffering does not mean every tragedy was desired or required. Love, freedom, lament, and responsible care remain part of the discussion.

Biblical miracle and deliverance accounts deserve attention to their narrative claims and purposes. Christians differ over personal, symbolic, and systemic readings of spiritual evil. The course does not treat a modern illness as evidence of possession or an unhealed condition as evidence of inadequate faith.

When someone is suffering, respond first with attention to immediate needs, listening, and appropriate help. Leave room for prayer and unanswered questions. Do not impose an explanation of God’s hidden intention or let a spiritual label displace practical care.

**Simply:** A tragedy does not prove its victims were worse people. Care can begin before every theological question is answered.

### Vocabulary

- **Moral evil:** Harm connected with wrongdoing and the use of human agency.
- **Natural suffering:** Suffering connected with disease or natural events rather than a directly identified malicious act.
- **Theodicy:** An attempt to reason about God’s goodness in relation to evil and suffering.

**Deeper:** The passage’s rejection of one inference does not answer every philosophical question. State both what it challenges and what remains unresolved.

**Optional reflection:** What can you responsibly do when you cannot explain why a loss occurred?

**Model reflection:** I can help with an immediate need and listen without claiming the loss reveals a hidden moral defect in the person.
