# Christianity foundations: curriculum and editorial framework

## Release scope

All sixteen units are available: 23 complete lessons, 50 initial understanding challenges, and 36 return-review challenges. Unit 1 contains five orientation lessons; Unit 2 contains four reading-method lessons; Units 3–16 each contain one integrated unit study with four primary-text encounters and layered explanation. This is a complete introductory path, not an exhaustive seminary course or a claim that every theological dispute has been resolved. The theological guide supplies sixteen approved topic explanations with interpretive boundaries. The full Bible reader, corpus search, original book drills, and saved progress remain available.

Every unit has a readable document generated from production lesson data and the Bible corpus. Documents include readings, explanations, vocabulary, reflections, challenge solutions, and review material. Run `npm run docs` after editing content.

## University-informed design

This is original curriculum, not copied course material, a university syllabus, academic credit, or an endorsed course. Concordia University Texas informs the Old/New Testament narrative foundations and guided questions. Bethel University in Mishawaka informs introductory vocabulary, historic theological positions, life connections, and contextual/inductive Bible study. The dated catalogs are sample course descriptions, not a claim about a current complete syllabus. Their denominational positions are not automatically adopted.

## Learning contract

Each lesson has an objective, full primary reading from corpus.txt (plus three additional primary readings in each integrated unit study), substantive explanation, a simpler explanation, vocabulary, deeper inquiry, optional reflection with a model, and at least two challenges. Readers may move freely. No profession of faith is required. Reflection is private and ungraded; exported progress includes reflections and says so. Learning milestones are separate from legacy arcade XP.

Challenges use story and reasoning sequencing, matching, evidence boards, directed argument maps, and multi-stage scenarios. Initial constructions are shuffled while preserving answer keys and keyboard operation. All support keyboard and touch without dragging or timers. Hints and retries are unlimited. Completing every challenge earns one milestone. Return practice after 1, 3, 7, then 14 days builds retention; intervals are product defaults, not a uniquely proven optimum. Early practice is available but cannot accelerate review awards. Unit 1 reviews revisit the original tasks with ordering variation. Unit 2 reviews use eight separately authored transfer problems. Units 3–16 add 28 review challenges that audit new claims and reconstruct conceptual connections. Reviews are formative practice, not a validated psychometric measure. Revisit a lesson and resume the same mission without losing its current construction. A milestone is evidence of task completion, not whole-topic mastery or personal belief.

## Complete scope and sequence

1. **Orientation:** Five lessons introducing the central story, library, narrative, context, and mercy.
2. **Reading Scripture (playable):** Genre (Psalm 23); audience and context (1 Corinthians 11:17–22); translation and manuscripts (1 Corinthians 13:1–7); responsible application (James 1:19–27). See [complete Unit 2](unit-02.md).
3. **Creation and humanity:** Genesis; image of God; sin and mortality; creation care.
4. **Covenant and liberation:** Abraham; Exodus; Sinai; covenant responsibilities.
5. **Land, kingdom, and exile:** Joshua and Judges; monarchy; temple; exile and return.
6. **Prayer and wisdom:** Psalms; Proverbs; Job; Ecclesiastes and Song of Songs.
7. **Prophets and justice:** Prophetic settings; judgment; hope; responsible application.
8. **Jesus in his Jewish world:** Second Temple setting; Gospels; kingdom and parables; discipleship.
9. **Cross and resurrection:** Passion narratives; sacrifice and atonement; resurrection; grace and faith.
10. **The early Church:** Acts; Paul’s letters; other letters; community and mission.
11. **God and Christian doctrine:** Trinity; incarnation; Spirit; providence and freedom.
12. **Practicing Christianity:** Baptism and Communion; prayer; mercy and justice; spiritual formation.
13. **Christian traditions:** Early creeds; major divisions; MCC and Disciples; ecumenical understanding.
14. **Difficult questions:** Suffering; ethical texts; sexuality and inclusion; other religions.
15. **Hope and new creation:** Resurrection; judgment; apocalyptic genre; renewed creation.
16. **Independent study:** Read a whole book; compare interpretations; build an evidence case; complete a synthesis project.

## Approved theological framework

### Reading Scripture

Scripture is inspired and authoritative, read through historical setting, literary genre, and the life and teaching of Jesus. This guide does not require inerrancy. Copying, translation, and interpretation can obscure meaning; differences must be examined with evidence rather than assumed to be deliberate corruption.

**Interpretive boundary / deeper study:** Compare what a passage says, how a translation renders it, and what an interpreter concludes. These are related but distinct questions.

**Read:** Luke 1:1–4; 2 Timothy 3:14–17.

### God and incarnation

Historic Christianity confesses one God in three persons and Jesus Christ as fully divine and fully human. These formulations seek to hold together biblical testimony about God, Jesus, and the Spirit. They are taught as central historical doctrine, with room for examination and questions.

**Interpretive boundary / deeper study:** One person acting in three roles and three separate gods are not equivalent to the historic formulation. Analogies have limits and should not be treated as definitions.

**Read:** Matthew 28:19; John 1:1–18; Philippians 2:5–11.

### Creation and human beginnings

Genesis presents humanity as created for life in relationship with God. Its literary and theological layers should be read in their ancient setting. The guide does not require young-earth creationism or assert that animals could not die before human sin.

**Interpretive boundary / deeper study:** Distinguish a scientific account of natural processes from a theological claim about purpose, dependence, and responsibility.

**Read:** Genesis 1–3; Psalm 104.

### Sin and mortality

Sin ruptures humanity’s relationship with God. Genesis portrays the loss of access to the tree of life. This guide emphasizes inherited mortality and a broken human condition without asserting inherited personal culpability for an ancestor’s act.

**Interpretive boundary / deeper study:** Christians differ over original sin, inherited guilt, and how to relate Adam to human origins. Read Genesis 3 and Romans 5 together while identifying where an interpretation goes beyond an explicit statement.

**Read:** Genesis 3:19–24; Romans 5:12–21.

### Christ’s saving work

Sin separates humanity from God and brings us under judgment. We cannot overcome sin and death or reconcile ourselves to God by our own efforts. Through his willing sacrifice and resurrection, Jesus bears our sins, confronts their consequences, defeats sin and death, and makes reconciliation with God possible.

**Interpretive boundary / deeper study:** Penal substitution explains Christ as bearing the penalty due to sinners. Victory, sacrifice, representation, and reconciliation are also important biblical themes. These accounts can overlap; no single mechanism is required here.

**Read:** Isaiah 53; Romans 3:21–26; 2 Corinthians 5:18–21; Colossians 2:13–15.

### Receiving salvation

Salvation is God’s gift of grace, made possible through Jesus Christ’s sacrifice and resurrection. We receive it by acknowledging our sin and inability to save ourselves, repenting, and trusting in Christ’s saving work. His sacrifice frees us from the burden of sin and restores our relationship with God.

**Interpretive boundary / deeper study:** Repentance and faith describe receiving the gift, not earning it. Inability to merit salvation does not mean a person lacks worth in God’s eyes. Love of neighbor belongs to discipleship and response to grace.

**Read:** Ephesians 2:8–10; Mark 1:15; Romans 5:1–11.

### Grace, freedom, and providence

God works through all circumstances toward his good purposes. Human beings make meaningful choices, including whether to accept or resist God’s grace. Those choices carry real consequences but cannot ultimately thwart God’s purpose. God’s loving pursuit does not make acceptance compulsory.

**Interpretive boundary / deeper study:** Foreknowledge, predestination, and determinism are different claims. Explain Reformed and other accounts fairly without presenting human response as an illusion.

**Read:** Romans 8:28–30; Ephesians 1:3–14; Matthew 23:37.

### Love, evil, and suffering

Love requires genuine freedom; freedom permits wrongdoing without making evil God’s desire. Divine intervention need not compel love or allegiance. Disease and disasters arise within a dynamic natural world; their scientific causes and theological meaning are distinct.

**Interpretive boundary / deeper study:** God can redeem suffering without every tragedy being a punishment or a lesson deliberately inflicted. Christianity offers multiple accounts of why suffering is permitted. Make room for uncertainty, lament, practical care, and hope.

**Read:** Job 1–2; Psalm 13; Luke 13:1–5; Romans 8:18–25.

### Baptism

Baptism marks belonging to Christ and a community, expressing union with his death and resurrection. The community undertakes to support the baptized person. This guide teaches its spiritual and communal significance without declaring it necessary for salvation.

**Interpretive boundary / deeper study:** Explain infant and believer’s baptism, sacramental and symbolic emphases, and differing accounts of its saving significance. Avoid reducing either practice to an empty custom.

**Read:** Romans 6:3–4; Acts 2:38–39.

### Communion

Communion remembers Christ’s sacrifice, expresses communion with him, and gathers people in unity. The guide affirms an open table: all are welcome, always, without a membership, baptism, or doctrinal-agreement requirement.

**Interpretive boundary / deeper study:** Explain real-presence, spiritual-presence, and memorial emphases fairly. Other churches have different admission practices; describe them accurately while making this guide’s invitation clear.

**Read:** Luke 22:14–20; 1 Corinthians 10:16–17; 11:17–34.

### LGBTQ dignity and belonging

LGBTQ people, their relationships and marriages, and their equal participation in leadership and ministry are explicitly affirmed. The guide’s MCC and Disciples-inspired direction makes inclusion an editorial commitment.

**Interpretive boundary / deeper study:** Explain opposing readings and affirming responses in context. Learners should understand interpretive arguments without being asked to debate whether another person has dignity or deserves care.

**Read:** Genesis 1:27; Matthew 22:34–40; Acts 10; Galatians 3:28.

### Judaism and other religions

Teach salvation through Christ while representing other faiths accurately and respectfully. Christianity’s Jewish roots are essential. Reject collective Jewish blame for Jesus’ death and do not declare that God abandoned the Jewish people.

**Interpretive boundary / deeper study:** Present disagreements about Israel’s continuing covenant and the fate of the unevangelized. Do not claim certainty about particular individuals’ final destiny. Personal reincarnation theories are not asserted teachings of this guide.

**Read:** Romans 9–11; John 4:22; Acts 17:22–31.

### Difficult ethical texts

Address conquest, slavery, and restrictions on women directly. Explain historical context and competing readings without making context an excuse for harm. Present-day application is examined through Jesus’ teaching, love of neighbor, and human dignity.

**Interpretive boundary / deeper study:** Distinguish a narrative’s report from an endorsement and an ancient legal arrangement from a timeless command. Admit where a passage raises unresolved moral questions.

**Read:** Joshua 6; Philemon; 1 Timothy 2; Luke 10:25–37.

### Prayer and healing

Prayer includes petition, gratitude, lament, and listening in relationship with God. Biblical miracles deserve substantive study alongside differing interpretations. Prayer is not a substitute for medical care.

**Interpretive boundary / deeper study:** Do not promise healing or blame illness on insufficient faith. Consider how a miracle functions within its narrative as well as the question of what occurred.

**Read:** Matthew 6:5–13; Mark 2:1–12; 2 Corinthians 12:7–10.

### Spiritual evil

Explain biblical portrayals of Satan, demons, and spiritual warfare, including personal, symbolic, and systemic interpretations. Teach discernment and responsibility without sensationalism.

**Interpretive boundary / deeper study:** Do not identify other groups as demonic or treat mental illness as evidence of possession. Keep textual interpretation distinct from diagnosing a living person.

**Read:** Mark 5:1–20; Ephesians 6:10–18.

### Resurrection and final hope

Bodily resurrection and renewed creation anchor Christian hope. Teach judgment, salvation through Christ, and eternal life substantively. Detailed end-times timelines are disputed interpretations.

**Interpretive boundary / deeper study:** Explain differing views of hell, separation from God, duration of punishment, and the unevangelized without making one disputed account settled. Repeated earthly lives and guaranteed opportunities after death are not curriculum assertions.

**Read:** 1 Corinthians 15; Revelation 21–22; Matthew 25:31–46.

## Covering difficult theology

Difficulty is not a universal empirical ranking. Prioritize conceptual complexity, interpretive disagreement, prerequisite knowledge, and pastoral consequences. For Trinity and incarnation, pair careful definitions with comparison of near-miss formulations; state analogy limits. For atonement, compare biblical themes and model explanations. For grace and freedom, distinguish foreknowledge, causation, and response using scenarios. For suffering, distinguish moral harm from natural processes, include lament and case-based care. For judgment, compare texts and interpretations without certainty about individuals. Every contested topic uses plain explanation, contextual primary passages, a comparison, and an application or evidence challenge as it is authored.

## Editorial and release gates

Approved positions are the guide’s stated perspective, not a claim that all Christians or all MCC/Disciples congregations agree. Describe competing positions in terms adherents would recognize. Cite sources and distinguish textual observation, historical claims, and interpretation. Never award points for assent, identity, or disclosure. No speculative personal cosmology is taught as doctrine. New substantive disagreements require owner review before publication. Future additions need complete prose, primary readings, aligned challenges, accessibility verification, and theological review before appearing as playable lessons.
