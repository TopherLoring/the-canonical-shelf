## Cross, resurrection, and the gift of grace

**Objective:** Distinguish Christ’s saving work, receiving grace, and the response of discipleship.

### 1 Corinthians 15:1–8 · Berean Standard Bible

**1** Now, brothers, I want to remind you of the gospel I preached to you, which you received, and in which you stand firm.

**2** By this gospel you are saved, if you hold firmly to the word I preached to you. Otherwise, you have believed in vain.

**3** For what I received I passed on to you as of first importance: that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures,

**4** that He was buried, that He was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures,

**5** and that He appeared to Cephas and then to the Twelve.

**6** After that, He appeared to more than five hundred brothers at once, most of whom are still living, though some have fallen asleep.

**7** Then He appeared to James, then to all the apostles.

**8** And last of all He appeared to me also, as to one of untimely birth.

The Christian proclamation holds Jesus’ death and resurrection together. Paul recalls Christ’s death for sins, burial, resurrection, and appearances. Christian faith does not treat the cross merely as an inspiring example of someone being kind. It proclaims a saving work humanity could not accomplish for itself.

Sin separates humanity from God and brings us under judgment. Through his willing sacrifice and resurrection, Jesus bears our sins, confronts their consequences, defeats sin and death, and makes reconciliation with God possible. The New Testament uses several themes to communicate this significance rather than only one explanatory picture.

Penal substitution describes Christ as bearing the penalty due to sinners. Other accounts emphasize victory over sin and death, sacrifice, representation, healing, and reconciliation. These themes can overlap. Our guide accommodates penal substitution without making its particular account of punishment the sole explanation of salvation.

Salvation is God’s gift of grace, made possible through Christ’s sacrifice and resurrection. We receive it by acknowledging sin and our inability to save ourselves, repenting, and trusting in Christ’s saving work. Repentance and faith describe receiving the gift rather than earning it. Inability to merit salvation does not mean a person lacks value in God’s eyes.

Ephesians connects grace with a life of good works while denying that salvation is a basis for boasting. Loving action belongs to the response to grace, not the price of admission. Keep the questions separate: what Christ accomplishes, how the gift is received, and how discipleship takes practical form. This distinction prevents both a merit contest and a faith reduced to words without responsibility.

**Simply:** Christ’s saving work makes reconciliation possible. Salvation is received as grace; loving action is its response, not its purchase price.

### Vocabulary

- **Atonement:** Christ’s work addressing sin and restoring relationship with God.
- **Reconciliation:** Restoration of a ruptured relationship.
- **Penal substitution:** An account in which Christ bears the penalty due to sinners.

**Deeper:** Compare the language of reconciliation in 2 Corinthians with victory in Colossians. What does each image illuminate, and what questions does it leave open?

**Optional reflection:** How would you explain the difference between receiving a gift and earning a reward?

**Model reflection:** Receiving involves accepting what is offered. Gratitude can produce action, but that response is not what makes the original offer a gift.

## Atonement: several images of one saving work

**Objective:** Distinguish reconciliation, substitution, and victory without reducing the cross to a moral example.

### 2 Corinthians 5:18–21 · Berean Standard Bible

**18** All this is from God, who reconciled us to Himself through Christ and gave us the ministry of reconciliation:

**19** that God was reconciling the world to Himself in Christ, not counting men’s trespasses against them. And He has committed to us the message of reconciliation.

**20** Therefore we are ambassadors for Christ, as though God were making His appeal through us. We implore you on behalf of Christ: Be reconciled to God.

**21** God made Him who knew no sin to be sin on our behalf, so that in Him we might become the righteousness of God.

This passage presents reconciliation as God’s initiative through Christ. Reconciliation concerns a relationship restored across estrangement. Its direction matters: the saving work is not described as something humanity first achieves by making itself worthy.

Paul also speaks of sin and righteousness in relation to Christ and those reconciled through him. Christians draw on this and other passages when explaining representation, substitution, sacrifice, and exchange. A theological model gathers evidence; it is not identical to a single metaphor.

Penal substitution describes Christ as bearing the penalty due to sinners. Victory accounts emphasize the defeat of sin and death; reconciliation emphasizes restored relationship. These themes can overlap. The guide accommodates penal substitution while declining to present it as the only scriptural account of how salvation is accomplished.

Christ’s necessary sacrifice and resurrection remain central. The lesson concerns what Christ accomplishes; the next lesson considers receiving the gift. Keeping these questions distinct protects the saving work from being reduced either to a transaction diagram or to an inspiring example humanity could reproduce on its own.

**Simply:** Reconciliation names restored relationship; penal substitution names bearing a penalty; victory names defeating hostile powers. Each highlights a dimension of Christ’s saving work.

- **Penal substitution:** An account in which Christ bears the penalty due to sinners.
- **Reconciliation:** The restoration of relationship across estrangement.

## Receiving grace: repentance and trust

**Objective:** Distinguish receiving salvation from earning it and human dependence from lack of human value.

### Ephesians 2:1–10 · Berean Standard Bible

**1** And you were dead in your trespasses and sins,

**2** in which you used to walk when you conformed to the ways of this world and of the ruler of the power of the air, the spirit who is now at work in the sons of disobedience.

**3** All of us also lived among them at one time, fulfilling the cravings of our flesh and indulging its desires and thoughts. Like the rest, we were by nature children of wrath.

**4** But because of His great love for us, God, who is rich in mercy,

**5** made us alive with Christ even when we were dead in our trespasses. It is by grace you have been saved!

**6** And God raised us up with Christ and seated us with Him in the heavenly realms in Christ Jesus,

**7** in order that in the coming ages He might display the surpassing riches of His grace, demonstrated by His kindness to us in Christ Jesus.

**8** For it is by grace you have been saved through faith, and this not from yourselves; it is the gift of God,

**9** not by works, so that no one can boast.

**10** For we are God’s workmanship, created in Christ Jesus to do good works, which God prepared in advance as our way of life.

Ephesians moves from a description of bondage and death toward God’s mercy and life with Christ. The movement gives divine initiative priority. It does not portray salvation as the final prize awarded to people who have already repaired themselves.

Grace names the gift-character of salvation. Faith names trust and reception rather than a competing currency with which to purchase it. In this guide, acknowledging sin, repentance, and acceptance of Christ’s saving gift belong to receiving grace, not to establishing a claim that God owes salvation.

The denial of boasting addresses the attempt to make salvation a measure of superiority. Inability to merit salvation must not be turned into the claim that people have no value. The text’s appeal to love and mercy belongs within the explanation, not outside it.

The movement toward good works remains part of the passage. The order matters: created for a changed life is not the same as saved by accumulating achievements. When explaining the gospel, separate the gift, its reception, and its ethical result while keeping their relationship visible.

**Simply:** Grace is a gift, faith receives it, and changed conduct follows. Receiving help does not mean a person has no value.

### Vocabulary

- **Grace:** God’s freely given favor and saving gift.
- **Faith:** Trust in and reception of God’s saving work through Christ.
- **Merit:** A claim to deserve a reward on the basis of achievement.

**Deeper:** Ask what boasting would imply about the source of salvation. Then ask why the passage still speaks of good works after denying that salvation is earned by works.

**Optional reflection:** How would you explain dependence on grace without humiliating someone?

**Model reflection:** I would say that none of us can accomplish salvation for ourselves, while emphasizing that God’s love gives people value rather than erasing it.

## After forgiveness: love takes practical form

**Objective:** Distinguish a response to grace from payment for welcome and salvation.

### Luke 19:1–10 · Berean Standard Bible

**1** Then Jesus entered Jericho and was passing through.

**2** And there was a man named Zacchaeus, a chief tax collector, who was very wealthy.

**3** He was trying to see who Jesus was, but could not see over the crowd because he was small in stature.

**4** So he ran on ahead and climbed a sycamore tree to see Him, since Jesus was about to pass that way.

**5** When Jesus came to that place, He looked up and said, “Zacchaeus, hurry down, for I must stay at your house today.”

**6** So Zacchaeus hurried down and welcomed Him joyfully.

**7** And all who saw this began to grumble, saying, “He has gone to be the guest of a sinful man!”

**8** But Zacchaeus stood up and said to the Lord, “Look, Lord, half of my possessions I give to the poor, and if I have cheated anyone, I will repay it fourfold.”

**9** Jesus said to him, “Today salvation has come to this house, because this man too is a son of Abraham.

**10** For the Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost.”

The Zacchaeus narrative joins seeking, encounter, welcome, complaint, and concrete concern about possessions and wrongdoing. Read the sequence before turning it into an abstract formula. Jesus’ approach and the crowd’s response are part of the meaning.

Zacchaeus speaks about giving and repayment. Readers debate aspects of the statement’s tense and implications, but its ethical content is clear: money and harm are not irrelevant to the encounter. The story concerns more than an inward feeling detached from neighbors.

This guide treats repair as a response to grace, not a payment that forces God to save. Repentance is more credible when it acknowledges those harmed. Forgiveness also does not give an offender the right to demand instant trust or unrestricted access to someone they have hurt.

A practical response can involve admission, restitution where possible, changed habits, and respect for boundaries. Different harms require different forms of repair. The lesson supplies a theological connection between grace and conduct, not a promise that every damaged relationship can be restored on demand.

**Simply:** Grace and practical repair belong together. Making amends is a response to the gift, not a way to buy it.

### Vocabulary

- **Restitution:** Returning what was taken or compensating for a concrete loss where possible.
- **Repentance:** Turning from wrongdoing in attitude and conduct.
- **Boundary:** A limit that can protect people even when forgiveness is being considered.

**Deeper:** Read the full encounter before treating one statement as a universal formula. Distinguish forgiveness, repaired material loss, and rebuilt trust; these are related but not identical.

**Optional reflection:** What makes an apology more than a request for relief from guilt?

**Model reflection:** It should acknowledge the harm and consider what repair the affected person needs. I cannot make their immediate trust a condition of my willingness to change.
