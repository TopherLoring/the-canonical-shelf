# Christianity foundations — curriculum and editorial framework

## Release scope

The Canonical Shelf now combines two complementary learning systems:

- **70 guided lessons in 16 units**, with 4–5 lessons in every unit.
- **69 integrated Bible-skill missions** rebuilt from the original Story, Order, Groups, Chronology, Content, Themes, and Verses tracks.

The application now presents these as **one curriculum**. Guided lessons develop biblical interpretation, Christian theology, history, practice, difficult questions, and independent study; embedded skill missions develop canonical orientation, book knowledge, chronological thinking, thematic connections, and verse-level recall/context at the point those skills are useful. The seven track names remain as competency dimensions, not separate courses.

The application also retains the complete Bible reader, corpus search, original book drills, saved progress, reflection notes, and progress import/export.

See [course-review.md](course-review.md) for the preservation audit, unit-by-unit expansion, strengths, known gaps, and recommended next improvements.

## University-informed design

This is original curriculum, not copied course material, a university syllabus, academic credit, or an endorsed course. Sample introductory course descriptions from Concordia University Texas informed the expectation that learners understand the broad Old/New Testament story and ask guided theological questions. Bethel University in Mishawaka informed the emphasis on introductory theological vocabulary, historic Christian positions, life connections, and contextual/inductive Bible study. Their denominational positions are not automatically adopted.

## Learning contract

Every guided lesson has an objective, a complete primary reading from `corpus.txt`, substantive explanation, a simpler explanation, vocabulary, a deeper layer, an optional private reflection with a model response, and an interactive understanding mission. Additional primary readings appear where comparison materially improves understanding.

Readers may move freely. No profession of faith is required. Reflection is private and ungraded; exported progress includes those reflections and says so. Learning milestones are distinct from book-drill XP.

Challenges use sequencing, matching, evidence boards, directed argument maps, and multi-stage scenarios. Hints and retries are unlimited. Missions are untimed. A challenge evaluates whether a learner can reconstruct an argument, identify supported evidence, recognize a position, or propose an application consistent with the lesson. It does **not** evaluate whether the learner personally assents to the position.

Return practice follows the existing 1-, 3-, 7-, and 14-day schedule. These intervals are product defaults rather than a claim of a uniquely proven optimum. A milestone records completion of the task, not comprehensive mastery of a subject or a judgment about faith.

## Original Bible-literacy curriculum

The original seven tracks have been remade as integrated skill missions within the revised learning path:

1. **The Story — 10 steps:** the biblical arc in ten beats, each checked against the books that carry it.
2. **Order — 17 steps:** group by group, each testament, then all 66 books.
3. **Groups — 12 steps:** the nine shelf groups plus reading approaches appropriate to the literary forms they contain.
4. **Chronology — 7 steps:** shelf order versus narrated/historical setting and composition. The timeline is a teaching model and disputed datings should be identified as such.
5. **Content — 8 steps:** summaries, major characters, openings, authorship information, and audiences.
6. **Themes — 7 steps:** fourteen thematic threads traced across books.
7. **Verses — 8 steps:** theme, speaker, recipient, and reconstruction of the verse.

The app's nine shelf groups are organizational categories, not nine perfectly uniform literary genres. A single book can contain narrative, law, poetry, genealogy, prophecy, correspondence, or symbolic vision. Likewise, shelf position, narrated setting, and date of composition answer different questions.

## Complete scope and sequence

1. **Orientation — 5 lessons:** central Christian proclamation; the Bible as a library; the larger biblical story; contextual reading; mercy and neighbor-love.
2. **Reading Scripture — 5 lessons:** genre; audience and social context; translation and textual criticism; observation → interpretation → application; shelf/timeline/original Bible-skill orientation.
3. **Creation and humanity — 4 lessons:** creation and image of God; garden/freedom; sin, mortality, and tree of life; creation care.
4. **Covenant and liberation — 4 lessons:** covenant overview; Abraham and blessing; Exodus/liberation; Torah and neighbor responsibility.
5. **Land, kingdom, and exile — 5 lessons:** integrated overview; Judges and difficult violence; monarchy/accountability; exile/lament; return/rebuilding.
6. **Prayer and wisdom — 5 lessons:** integrated overview; lament in Psalms; discernment in Proverbs; Job and suffering; Ecclesiastes and Song of Songs.
7. **Prophets and justice — 4 lessons:** prophetic overview; Amos and worship/justice; Micah and mercy; Jeremiah and exilic hope.
8. **Jesus in his Jewish world — 4 lessons:** integrated Gospel overview; kingdom/discipleship; parables/welcome; comparing Gospel presentations.
9. **Cross and resurrection — 4 lessons:** integrated saving-work overview; atonement images; receiving grace; repentance and practical repair.
10. **The early Church — 4 lessons:** integrated Acts/letters overview; Acts 15 and communal discernment; body/belonging; James and favoritism.
11. **God and Christian doctrine — 5 lessons:** integrated doctrinal overview; Trinity; incarnation; Spirit; providence, foreknowledge, freedom, and responsibility.
12. **Practicing Christianity — 4 lessons:** integrated practice overview; baptism; Communion/open table; prayer and formation.
13. **Christian traditions — 4 lessons:** integrated traditions overview; creeds; authority/polity/ecumenism; comparison of ELCA, UCC, UMC, MCC, Episcopal, Disciples, and Alliance of Baptists.
14. **Difficult questions — 5 lessons:** integrated difficult-questions overview; slavery/other difficult ethical texts; LGBTQ affirmation and interpretive disagreement; Jewish roots/religious difference; suffering, miracles, and spiritual discernment.
15. **Hope and new creation — 4 lessons:** integrated final-hope overview; bodily resurrection; judgment and competing final-destiny interpretations; apocalyptic symbolism and renewed creation.
16. **Independent study — 4 lessons:** integrated project overview; whole-book observation/profile; argument mapping and theme tracing; application, revision, and continued learning.

## Approved theological framework

### Reading Scripture

Scripture is inspired and authoritative and is read through historical setting, literary genre, textual evidence, and the life and teaching of Jesus. This guide does not require biblical inerrancy. Copying, translation, and interpretation can introduce errors or obscure meaning; differences should be examined rather than automatically attributed either to deliberate corruption or to perfect preservation of every interpretation.

**Interpretive boundary:** textual evidence, translation choice, and theological interpretation are related but distinct questions. The 1 John 5:6–8 workshop gives learners a concrete example of a manuscript-variant decision.

### God, Trinity, and incarnation

Historic Christianity confesses one God in three persons—Father, Son, and Holy Spirit—and Jesus Christ as fully divine and fully human. These formulations draw together biblical testimony and are taught as central historical Christian doctrine while leaving room for examination and questions.

**Interpretive boundary:** three separate gods, one person merely switching roles, or Jesus as a half-divine/half-human mixture are not equivalent to the historic formulations. Analogies have limits and must be identified as analogies.

### Creation and human beginnings

Genesis portrays creation as God's work and humanity as created for life in relationship with God, bearing dignity and responsibility. The text can contain historical, literary, theological, conceptual, and metaphorical dimensions appropriate to its ancient context. The course does not require young-earth creationism or claim that animal death was absent before human sin.

**Interpretive boundary:** scientific accounts of natural processes and theological claims about purpose, dependence, dignity, and responsibility should not be collapsed into one kind of explanation.

### Sin and mortality

Sin ruptures humanity's relationship with God. Genesis depicts distrust, alienation, mortality, and loss of access to the tree of life. The course emphasizes inherited mortality and a broken human condition without teaching that a person is personally culpable merely for an ancestor's act.

**Interpretive boundary:** Christian traditions differ on original sin, inherited guilt, and the relation between Adam and human origins. Mortality and personal guilt are not synonyms.

### Christ's saving work

Sin separates humanity from God and places humanity under judgment. Human beings cannot overcome sin and death or reconcile themselves to God through their own achievement. Through his willing sacrifice and resurrection, Jesus bears sin, confronts its consequences, defeats sin and death, and makes reconciliation with God possible.

**Interpretive boundary:** penal substitution is a significant Christian account in which Christ bears the penalty due to sinners. Scripture also uses sacrifice, representation, reconciliation, liberation, and victory language. These can overlap; penal substitution is accommodated without being the only permitted explanatory model.

### Receiving salvation

Salvation is God's gift of grace, made possible through Jesus Christ's sacrifice and resurrection. It is received by acknowledging sin and human inability to save ourselves, repentance, trust in Christ's saving work, and acceptance of the gift. His sacrifice frees us from the burden of sin and restores relationship with God.

**Interpretive boundary:** repentance and faith describe receiving the gift rather than earning it. Human inability to merit salvation does not mean human beings lack value. Love, justice, repair, and good works belong to Christian response and discipleship rather than serving as a price paid for salvation.

### Grace, freedom, and providence

God works through circumstances toward good purposes. Human choices remain meaningful, including response to grace; those choices have genuine consequences without ultimately defeating God's purpose. God's loving pursuit does not make acceptance compulsory.

**Interpretive boundary:** foreknowledge, predestination, providence, and determinism are different claims. Knowing an outcome does not mean the same thing as causing every choice. God bringing good from harm does not make the harmful act good.

### Love, evil, and suffering

Love requires meaningful freedom; freedom allows wrongdoing without making evil something God requires or desires. Divine intervention need not automatically eliminate freedom. Disease, disaster, and other natural suffering arise within a dynamic natural world; their scientific causes and theological meaning are distinct questions.

**Interpretive boundary:** God may redeem suffering without every tragedy being a punishment or a lesson deliberately inflicted. The course makes room for lament, uncertainty, practical care, and hope and does not blame illness on insufficient faith.

### Baptism

Baptism marks belonging to Christ and Christian community and is associated with participation in Christ's death and resurrection. It is spiritually and socially meaningful; communities make commitments to support those baptized. This guide does not declare baptism necessary for salvation.

**Interpretive boundary:** infant baptism, believer's baptism, sacramental accounts, symbolic emphases, and claims about baptism's saving significance are represented fairly rather than flattened into one practice.

### Communion

Communion remembers Christ's sacrifice, expresses participation in his life, and gathers people in unity. In this guide the table is open: **all are welcome, always**, without requiring membership, baptism, or doctrinal agreement.

**Interpretive boundary:** real-presence, spiritual-presence, and memorial accounts are distinct and should be explained in terms their adherents would recognize. Other churches may have different admission practices.

### LGBTQ dignity and belonging

LGBTQ people, relationships, marriage, and equal participation in leadership and ministry are explicitly affirmed. This is a settled editorial commitment shaped especially by the course's MCC and Disciples orientation.

**Interpretive boundary:** opposing biblical interpretations can be explained and examined contextually. Learners are not asked to debate whether another participant possesses dignity or deserves care.

### Judaism and other religions

Christianity's Jewish roots are indispensable to understanding Jesus, Paul, Scripture, covenant, and the earliest Church. The course rejects collective Jewish blame for Jesus' death and simplistic claims that God abandoned the Jewish people. It teaches salvation through Christ while representing other faiths accurately and respectfully.

**Interpretive boundary:** Christians disagree about Israel's continuing covenant and the fate of people who have not understood the Christian proclamation. The course does not claim knowledge of a particular person's final destiny and does not teach personal reincarnation theories as scriptural doctrine.

### Difficult ethical texts

Conquest, slavery, restrictions on women, and other difficult texts are addressed directly. Historical context can explain a setting without automatically excusing harm. Readers distinguish a narrative report from endorsement, an ancient legal arrangement from a timeless command, and an inference from an explicit statement.

**Interpretive boundary:** present-day applications are examined through the wider scriptural conversation, the life and teaching of Jesus, love of neighbor, and human dignity. Some moral questions remain difficult after careful study.

### Prayer, miracles, and healing

Prayer includes petition, gratitude, lament, confession, and listening within relationship with God. Biblical miracle accounts receive substantive study and attention to their role in the narratives.

**Interpretive boundary:** prayer is not a substitute for medical care. The curriculum does not guarantee healing, blame illness on insufficient faith, or treat an unhealed condition as evidence of spiritual failure.

### Spiritual evil

Biblical portrayals of Satan, demons, and spiritual warfare are examined, including personal, symbolic, and systemic interpretations. Discernment and responsibility are emphasized without sensationalism.

**Interpretive boundary:** another group should not be casually identified as demonic, and mental illness is not treated as evidence of possession. Textual interpretation must remain distinct from diagnosing a living person.

### Resurrection, judgment, and final hope

Bodily resurrection and renewed creation anchor Christian final hope. Judgment, salvation through Christ, the defeat of death, and eternal life are taught substantively. Detailed end-times timelines remain disputed interpretations.

**Interpretive boundary:** Christians differ on the nature/duration of final punishment, conditional immortality/final destruction, universal reconciliation, and the unevangelized. Resurrection is distinct from repeated earthly reincarnation. The course does not guarantee postmortem opportunities or claim certainty about an individual's final destiny.

## Denominational orientation

The course gives particular editorial weight to Metropolitan Community Churches and the Christian Church (Disciples of Christ): radical welcome, open Communion, Christian unity, contextual reading, freedom of inquiry, LGBTQ affirmation, and concern for justice. It also introduces the ELCA, United Church of Christ, United Methodist Church, Episcopal Church, and Alliance of Baptists so learners can distinguish theological center, authority, sacramental practice, and governance rather than assuming all progressive churches are the same.

Official denominational sources are linked from the relevant lesson. A denominational statement is not assumed to describe every local congregation; learners are encouraged to ask concrete questions about actual practice.

## Covering difficult theology

Topics become difficult for different reasons: conceptual complexity, historical distance, disagreement among Christians, translation/textual questions, prerequisite knowledge, or pastoral consequences. The teaching method therefore uses more than one explanation:

- precise definitions and near-miss comparisons for Trinity/incarnation;
- multiple biblical images and comparison for atonement;
- causal/foreknowledge distinctions and scenarios for providence/freedom;
- lament, case-based care, and moral/natural distinctions for suffering;
- textual criticism workshops for transmission/translation;
- genre comparisons for poetry, narrative, law, letters, and apocalypse;
- argument maps that expose the path from observation to interpretation to application;
- fair comparison of disputed positions without grading personal assent.

## Editorial and release gates

Approved positions are the guide's stated perspective, not a claim that every Christian or every MCC/Disciples congregation agrees. Competing positions must be described in recognizable terms. Sources, textual observations, historical reconstruction, theological interpretation, and application should be distinguishable. No learner receives points for identity, religious disclosure, or professing a required belief. New substantive theological disagreements require owner review before they are presented as the guide's settled position.

The full post-expansion review identifies further priorities: harder late-course transfer work, a scholarly source/confidence audit of all 66 book profiles, human feedback on independent-study projects, deeper Second Temple/canon history, visual orientation tools, and target-learner/accessibility testing.
